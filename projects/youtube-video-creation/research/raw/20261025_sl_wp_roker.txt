'''Roker Park''' was a [[Football (soccer)|football]] ground in [[Roker]], [[Sunderland, Tyne and Wear|Sunderland]], England, which was the [[List of Sunderland A.F.C. grounds|home]] of [[Sunderland A.F.C.|Sunderland]] from 1898 to 1997, before the club moved to the [[Stadium of Light]].

The stadium's capacity varied throughout its existence. Originally designed to accommodate 60,000 attendees, its capacity was gradually reduced to around 22,500, of which only a small proportion consisted of seated places. Its highest recorded attendance was, however, significantly higher, with the [[1932–33 FA Cup]] match on 4 March 1933 attracting a record crowd of 75,118.<ref>{{cite web

==History==
[[File:Wearmouth Bridge to the Sea 2nd March 1967.jpg|thumbnail|left|Roker Park (top left) pictured from above in 1967]]
In the 1890s, the then [[Sunderland A.F.C.|Sunderland]] chairman and his brother decided to build a bigger ground for the club, to replace what was then the club's current ground at [[Newcastle Road (stadium)|Newcastle Road]]. The club had negotiated to buy farmland that belonged to a Mr. Tennant and part of the agreement was that Sunderland would have to build a house on the site as well as their new stadium. Until this house was built, Sunderland still had to pay rent on the land.

Within a year of the land being bought, Roker Park had been built, with the wooden stands only taking three months to build. The Clock Stand had 32 steps, no seats and a [[crush barrier]] for safety. The turf was brought from Ireland, and lasted for 38 years. The pitch was designed to have a slight drop of about one foot from the centre of the pitch to each corner to help with drainage. The first event at the ground was an "Olympic Games and Band Contest" on 12 August 1898. On 10 September 1898, Roker Park was officially opened by [[Charles Vane-Tempest-Stewart, 6th Marquess of Londonderry]]. The first match was a friendly against [[Liverpool F.C.|Liverpool]] which Sunderland won 1–0, with [[Jim Leslie (footballer)|Jim Leslie]] scoring the stadium's first ever goal.<ref>{{cite news |first=Rob |last=Mason |url=https://www.chroniclelive.co.uk/sport/football/football-news/sunderlands-roker-park-remembered-roar-15147389 |title=Sunderland's Roker Park remembered - the roar, the stars, the big games 120 years after first match |website=Chronicle Live |date=13 September 2018 }}</ref>

The stadium soon became famous for what was often referred to as the "Roker Roar", with then [[Tottenham Hotspur F.C.|Tottenham]] and [[Northern Ireland national football team|Northern Ireland]] captain [[Danny Blanchflower]] writing that “Nothing ever equalled the intensity of that wild roar at Roker Park”.<ref>{{Cite web |date=2019-06-01 |title=McPheat's feat |url=https://www.thenorthernecho.co.uk/sport/columnists/backtrack/backtrack/17668425.something-approaching-200-decibels-column-joins-roker-roar/ |access-date=2023-09-12 |website=The Northern Echo |language=en}}</ref>

The Roker End was concreted in 1912, and by 1913 the capacity had risen to 50,000. In 1929 the old wooden grandstand was demolished and replaced by a new Main Stand, which was designed by [[Archibald Leitch]], whose influence, the criss-cross lattice work, can still be seen at [[Ibrox Stadium|Ibrox]] ([[Rangers F.C.|Rangers]]), [[Home Park]] ([[Plymouth Argyle F.C.|Plymouth Argyle]]) and [[Goodison Park]] ([[Everton F.C.|Everton]]). Fragments of the iconic lattice work would later be used to separate parts of the car park at the [[Stadium of Light]]. The work on the new Main Stand nearly bankrupted the club. By this time the official capacity of Roker Park was 60,000 but at some matches they would have crowds as large as 75,000. More work continued in the 1930s and in 1936 the Clock Stand was rebuilt. The 114&nbsp;m (375&nbsp;ft) long structure was officially opened by Lady Raine, whose husband was Sir Walter Raine, the Chairman of Sunderland AFC at the time.
[[File:Leitch lattice work stadium of light.jpg|left|thumb|Fragments of the [[Archibald Leitch]] lattice work from the Main Stand now form part of the car park at the [[Stadium of Light]].]]
In 1943, during the [[Second World War]], a bomb landed in the middle of the pitch and another nearby killed a policeman.<ref>{{cite book |first=Anton |last=Rippon |title=Gas Masks for Goal Posts: Football in Britain during the Second World War |location=Stroud |publisher=Sutton |year=2007 |orig-year=2005 |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=raoTDQAAQBAJ&pg=PT79 |isbn=9780750940313 }}</ref> In 1952, Roker Park was fitted with floodlights, being only the second ground in the country to do so after [[Arsenal F.C.|Arsenal]]'s [[Arsenal Stadium|Highbury]]. The lights were only a temporary addition, and were replaced by permanent structures at the end of the season after proving to be a success. When England hosted the [[1966 World Cup]], improvements were made to the Clock Stand, involving the addition of seats, and a roof over the Fulwell End. Temporary seating was also installed in the Fulwell End. The capacity of Roker Park during the World Cup was 40,310.

In 1955, Roker Park hosted an [[FA Cup]] semi-final replay, between (eventual winners) [[Newcastle United F.C.|Newcastle United]] and [[York City F.C.|York City]], who were then in the third tier of English football. Newcastle won the game 2–0 in front of a crowd of 55,239.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.11v11.com/matches/newcastle-united-v-york-city-30-march-1955-210704/|title=Newcastle United v York City, 30 March 1955|work=11v11.com|access-date=2018-11-09}}</ref> Sunderland were in the other semi-final, but lost 1–0 to [[Manchester City F.C.|Manchester City]].

During the 1970s, there were even more improvements to Roker Park. These improvements included installing underground sprinklers, upgrading the floodlights to European Standard Lux Value, installing electronic crowd monitoring systems, and re-sheeting the roof. In the 1980s, with a downturn in the club's fortunes (which included a season in the [[Football League Third Division]]), Roker Park started to decline. The capacity was severely reduced following the report in the [[Hillsborough Disaster]] of 1989, with the Roker End suffering the most.

In the early 1990s, new [[The Football Association|Football Association]] (FA) rules following the publication of the [[Taylor Report]] meant the stadium would have to have been upgraded to all-seater status, which would have consisted of a much smaller capacity than the sort of attendances that Sunderland could expect, as they had played in the penultimate [[Football League First Division|First Division]] campaign ([[1990–91 in English football|1990–91 season]]) before the creation of the [[Premier League]] and were aiming for a swift return to the top flight (although promotion was ultimately not achieved until the [[1995–96 in English football|1995–96 season]]). The site was too confined for expansion<ref>{{cite web
|url=http://www.safc.com/history/?page_id=2710
|title= Previous grounds: Roker Park, Sunderland
|access-date=23 December 2008
|publisher=SAFC.COM
}}</ref> so chairman [[Bob Murray (businessman)|Bob Murray]] decided to look for a site for a new stadium. In 1992, plans were unveiled to build a 48,000-seat stadium near the [[Nissan]] [[NMUK|car factory]] in [[Washington, Tyne and Wear|Washington]], which would be part of a mega leisure complex, but [[Nissan]] objected to such a site being developed near their factory. Instead, five years later in 1997, Sunderland moved to the [[Stadium of Light]], in nearby [[Monkwearmouth]], on the site of the closed Monkwearmouth Colliery.

The 1996–97 season was the last at Roker Park, which was also Sunderland's first ever season in the [[Premier League]], ending in relegation for the club.<ref>{{cite web
|url=http://www.safc.com/history/?page_id=2735
|title= Sunderland History 1990-1999
|access-date=23 December 2008
|publisher=SAFC.COM
}}</ref> The last competitive match at the ground was a 3–0 victory over [[Everton F.C.|Everton]].

Roker Park was also one of the most recent venues in the Premier League to feature standing accommodation. The only team to have had standing accommodation at its stadium since was [[Fulham F.C.|Fulham]], who still had terracing at [[Craven Cottage]] for one season after winning promotion in 2001, after which they ground-shared with [[Queens Park Rangers F.C.|QPR]] at [[Loftus Road]] for two seasons before returning to an all-seater Craven Cottage.

In a special ceremony after the final farewell game (coincidentally, also a 1–0 win against [[Liverpool F.C.|Liverpool]], in which Sunderland midfielder [[John Mullin (footballer)|John Mullin]] scored the stadium's final goal at the Fulwell End), [[Charlie Hurley]] (voted the club's Player of the Century) dug up the centre spot of the ground for it to be planted at the new stadium. Following the move to the [[Stadium of Light]], Roker Park was demolished and in its place was built a housing estate. To commemorate Roker Park, the streets were named Promotion Close, Clockstand Close, Goalmouth Close, Midfield Drive, Turnstile Mews and Roker Park Close.<ref>{{cite book

==Fan reactions to the move==
Following the demolition of Roker Park, playwright Tom Kelly and actor Paul Dunn created a one-man play called ''I Left My Heart at Roker Park'' about a fan struggling with the move and what Roker Park meant for him; the play had its first run in 1997, and has been revived since.<ref>{{cite web |url=https://rokerreport.sbnation.com/2014/8/11/5990401/i-left-my-heart-at-roker-park-roker-report-meets-paul-dunn |title='I Left My Heart in Roker Park' - Roker Report Meets Paul Dunn |website=Roker Report |publisher=SB Nation |date=11 August 2014 }}</ref><ref>{{cite web |url=https://thepauldunn.co.uk/projects/past-projects/2014-2/i-left-my-heart-in-roker-park-by-tom-kelly/ |title=I Left My Heart in Roker Park by Tom Kelly |website=PaulDunn.co.uk |date=July 2014 }}</ref><ref>{{cite web |first=Peter |last=Lathan |url=https://www.britishtheatreguide.info/reviews/rokerpark-rev |title=I Left My Heart in Roker Park... (And Extra Time at the Stadium of Light) |website=British Theatre Guide |date=2004 }}</ref><ref>{{cite news |url=https://www.sunderlandecho.com/news/share-your-stadium-of-light-tales-357411 |title=Share your Stadium of Light tales |newspaper=Sunderland Echo |date=12 May 2017 |orig-year=11 May 2017 }}</ref>

Actor and Sunderland supporter [[Peter O'Toole]] described Roker Park as his connection to the club, saying that as a result of the change of grounds he was less of a fan.<ref>{{cite web |first=Davey |last=Edmondson |url=https://bleacherreport.com/articles/855822-english-premier-league-each-clubs-most-famous-fans |title=English Premier League: Each Club's Most Famous Fans. Sunderland: Peter O'Toole |website=Bleacher Report |date=19 September 2011 }}</ref><ref>{{cite web |first=Colin |last=Randall |url=https://safc.blog/peter-otoole-and-a-lost-sunderland-passion/ |title=Peter O'Toole and a lost Sunderland passion |website=SAFC.COM |date=14 September 2010 }}</ref>

In 1998, the BBC broadcast a six-part documentary named ''[[Premier Passions]]'', which included a chronicle of Sunderland during the [[1996–97 in English football|1996–97 season]], in which the club was [[Promotion and relegation|relegated]] from the [[Premier League]] the year after winning [[promotion and relegation|promotion]] from the [[Football League First Division]], and moved to the [[Stadium of Light]].<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://www.imdb.com/title/tt0364860/plotsummary|title=Premier Passions (TV Series 1998– ) |website=IMDb}}</ref><ref>{{Cite news |url=http://www.chroniclelive.co.uk/sport/football/football-news/sunderlands-premier-passions-remembered-20-13166765|title=Sunderland's Premier Passions remembered 20 years after fly-on-the-wall TV came to Roker Park|first=James|last=Hunter|date=11 June 2017|website=Chronicle Live }}</ref>

== International matches ==
Roker Park hosted the first of three full [[England national football team|England]] internationals in 1899; a 13–2 victory over [[Ireland national football team (1882–1950)|Ireland]] in the [[1898–99 British Home Championship]] - the highest scoring game in the history of the tournament. Further Home Championship games were played at Roker Park in 1920 (v. Ireland) and 1950 (v. Wales), England winning both games. Three [[England national football B team|England B]] games were played at the ground, in 1954, 1980 and 1990. Roker Park also hosted four youth internationals - [[England national under-21 football team|England U23]] games in 1959 and 1970, and [[England national under-18 football team|England U18]] games in 1952 and 1956. The 1970 England U23 game against Scotland was abandoned due to snow after 60 minutes with England leading 3–1.

During the [[1966 FIFA World Cup]], Roker Park hosted three group stage games in Group 4 along with [[Ayresome Park]], and a Quarter Final game between the [[Soviet Union national football team|Soviet Union]] and [[Hungary national football team|Hungary]]. The biggest World Cup attendance at the ground was 27,793 for the group stage game between the [[Soviet Union national football team|Soviet Union]] and [[Italy national football team|Italy]].

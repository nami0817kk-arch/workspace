Stadium is no white elephant but future is just a guess
This article is more than 18 years old
City of Manchester Stadium architect Dipesh Patel weighs up the pros and cons of the 2012 Olympic centrepiece.
The Guardian
Thu 8 Nov 2007 00.04 GMT
Share
Prefer the Guardian on Google
Too many Olympic venues have turned into white elephants despite all the talk about their regenerative potential. Athens, Sydney, Barcelona and Beijing invested heavily in creating landmarks for their cities, but the first two stadiums are used infrequently, Barcelona's venue is not used to capacity and time will tell if Beijing can make regular use of its new icon.
Olympic architecture has become equated with iconic architecture to such an extent that we do not question whether this is actually the best way to do it, in terms of creating genuine atmosphere at events and creating a venue with a financially and socially viable legacy. The legacy revealed yesterday for London's 2012 Olympic stadium shows a different approach: a combined athletics and football/rugby facility of 25,000 fixed seats. In many ways it will be similar to the existing Crystal Palace athletics stadium. But it may be difficult to provide a facility that meets the requirements of the two groups of fans. Attracting a football or rugby club to a venue with a track is a challenge.
Manchester City Council's approach to the Commonwealth Games allowed us to design the venue to the legacy user's requirements because we knew who they were: 
Manchester
 City FC. If there is no confirmed legacy tenant the only option is to second-guess the future tenants' needs and aspirations. This is what London has had to do. The adoption of a demountable approach is very brave, and may turn out to be a major legacy to the whole Olympic movement by turning the tide of building expensive icons. A design reminiscent of big tops and festivals will generate an atmosphere around the Olympics that many cities have tried to graft on to vast permanent structures. But is leaving a shadow of the former stadium, in a form that replicates existing underused facilities, the best legacy? Might it be better to take the temporary concept to its logical conclusion and relocate the whole venue to another city or country?
The 2012 legacy proposal seems to be a sunken, continuous single tier of seating at a constant height with a capacity of 25,000. At this scale atmosphere needs to be a fundamental consideration in the design and simply putting 25,000 people around a track or pitch does not necessarily generate that. Perhaps the most crucial consideration is intimacy followed closely by proximity.
An athletics track and a football or rugby pitch are very different in size and combining them in one stadium is problematic. Our football fans demand very close proximity to the touchline. Watching football across a track is no longer the norm. This trend is also taking hold in mainland Europe: Bayern Munich left the incredible Munich Olympic Stadium because it did not meet their fans' viewing aspirations.
There are only two options for dual athletics and football use at a modern stadium. The Stade de France employs a moving lower tier to overcome the proximity problems for football and rugby by moving the lower tier over the track. At the City of Manchester Stadium we employed the "dig-down" approach to convert the Commonwealth Games athletics stadium into a Premier League venue. Although this created an exemplary football venue, it can no longer be used for athletics; however, the adjacent warm-up track was turned into an ideal regional athletics venue with 6,000 seats.
I applaud the 2012 approach for its simplicity: a structure for the Games with the bare essentials of seating, a track and a partial roof with all other elements such as toilets, bars and merchandising in service "pods" - self-contained structures outside the stadium. They are a brilliant idea which should prove to be a very useful legacy across London and perhaps nationally. They could easily be put to work in the parks for the festivals and other events.
This should be very cost effective and will encourage less well-off countries in Africa and Asia to host the Olympics if the trend takes hold. And it is atmosphere which ultimately makes a venue popular and results in exciting events. An 80,000-seat stadium, of any design, filled with people is always a thrilling and memorable experience. A utilitarian approach will not stop this design accompanying a successful Olympics.
Dipesh Patel was architect of Manchester's Commonwealth Games stadium and the Barbados cricket ground, which last week won the Sports Building of the Year award from Building Design magazine
